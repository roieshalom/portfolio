Context

BuildingMinds was building a platform for real estate portfolios. The platform ran on data about every piece of equipment in every room of every building, and that data came from a person walking the building with a clipboard.

We started an internal App Factory to build the tools that fed it. EQ Capture was the first one, and I co-founded it as its only designer.

The Problem

A surveyor arrives with a paper map, a pen, a digital camera and two spare batteries. He photographs each pump, each panel, each lift. Then he photographs the paper map, so that later he will remember which room he was standing in.

Then he drives back. Two days at two screens, matching photographs to rooms from memory, typing everything into Excel.

The platform's data started life as a spreadsheet reconstructed after the fact.

Surveyors at work: capturing equipment data on rooftops and in technical rooms across Hamburg and Frankfurt.

The old workflow ended in a spreadsheet. The new one feeds directly into BuildingMinds' platform.

Discovery

Learning through the feet

Around ten building visits over the project. Basements, plant rooms, roofs, whatever the day held.

The capture problem we already knew about. The sorting problem we found by accident. I invited Oliver, one of the surveyors, to the office, mostly to watch him work, and the two days at two screens appeared. He had never raised it. To him it was not a problem, it was Tuesday.

That is the part of the job the product ended up removing entirely.

Job shadowing: crawling through hatches, reviewing floor plans, observing the full capture workflow firsthand.

The full 'as-is' process: preparation, on-site data collection, and post-survey consolidation mapped end to end.

Key Decisions
01

I killed my own assumption

I was certain surveyors needed their location pinned to the screen at all times. Testing said the opposite. They always know where they are, that is the one thing they never lose track of. Location stopped being a display and became a default the form filled in by itself.

02

Calming down the keyboard

The first form followed the order of the Excel columns, which meant keyboard up, keyboard down, camera, keyboard again, for every single item. We reordered the fields by how you enter them: camera first, then pickers, then typing. Same data, one keyboard.

03

Fifteen sheets of paper taped around the office

I printed fake equipment labels, taped them to walls and desks, and ran the capture flow end to end. That is where the keyboard problem surfaced. Colleagues who had never seen the app ran it too, and showed us which field labels meant nothing to anyone outside the team.

Picking a classification code

Every item has to be classified against a code list of several hundred entries. I explored three ways in:

  • Filter by prefix. Choose a category, then type the digits you know.
  • Browse categories. Open the tree level by level.
  • Search fragments. Type any digits you remember, match anywhere in the code.

None of them won on its own, because surveyors were all three people on the same day. Someone who has classified pumps for fifteen years wants to type 412.13 and move on. Someone in their first month needs to see what exists. The shipped control let you type straight in if you knew the code and open the list if you did not.

The Product

Three screens, one trip through a building.

Your buildings. What is assigned to you, how far each survey has got, what is left. A surveyor opens the app in a car park and knows where to start.

Spaces. Floors and rooms as the building is actually organised, with the equipment count sitting on each one. This is the screen that replaced photographing the paper map.

Capture. One equipment record, grouped by what you are doing rather than by what the database wants: identify it, classify it, photograph it, note anything odd. Required and optional are visible. The save state tells you where you are before you walk to the next room.

Reflection

The app shipped and ran real surveys before a change in the partnership behind it brought the project to an end.

What I would carry into anything else: the field work paid for itself many times over. Nothing we built that mattered came out of a workshop. It came out of standing in a plant room watching someone work around a problem they had stopped noticing.

What I would do differently is slower to see. The product was meant to feel like its own thing and also like part of the platform, and I kept designing through that question instead of getting it answered. Positioning does not resolve in Figma. I would put that decision in front of the founders in week two and design after it rather than around it.

And one thing I got right by accident. My co-founders had never worked with a designer. I never argued the case, I just did the work in front of them, and at the end they told me it was the first time they understood what a designer was for. Good outcome. Next time I would make that visible on purpose, and earlier, rather than hoping it lands by the finish.
